# Dan Bogart, "Securing the East India Monopoly: Politics, Institutional Change, and the Security of British Property Rights Revisited", NBER conference draft, Oct 2014; published as "The East Indian Monopoly and the Transition from Limited Access in England, 1600–1813", in N. Lamoreaux & J. Wallis (eds.), *Organizations, Civil Society, and the Roots of Development* (Chicago, 2017), 23–49

- Type: SECONDARY. Language: English.
- Fetched: https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f75637/f75637.pdf ; https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13506.pdf

## Extracts (short quotes)

- 1730: rival-company petition "failed in the Commons by a vote of 223 to 138"; the act
  extended the monopoly (to at least 1769) in return for £200,000 to George II and a lower
  interest rate on the £3.2m owed by government.
- 1744: "In 1744 the Company was forced to lend £1,000,000 to King George II at 3 percent
  interest", in return for an extension of privileges (to at least 1780/1783).
- Using monthly EIC and VOC prices 1743–46 (from Van Dillen): "The English East India
  Company's price falls relative to the Dutch price immediately before the bill passes";
  "The relative decline in the share price of the English Company in the four months
  following March is 9%."
- "The average daily share price from January 1, 1715 to December 30, 1750 was 168.3."
- Company borrowing rate "dropping below 6% after 1719 and falling to as low as 3% in the
  early 1740s."
- Forced loans in about 3% of years 1721–1750 (vs 7% in 1691–1720); mid-1720s greater
  political stability; government debt to the Company £4.2m for rest of century.

## Used for

EIC's political risk (1730 renewal cost, 1744 forced loan and the 9% relative dip), EIC
long-run price level (168.3), and the overall security of EIC property rights after the 1720s.
This is also the main *counter-evidence* I found against EIC (see notes/05).
