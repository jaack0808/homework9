# 05 — Refutation pass

The assignment asks for the draft to be fed back to an online chatbot with web search, looking
for sources that *refute* its conclusions. Draft 1 was given to the chatbot (Claude, with web
search). This file records the challenges posed, what was searched, what was found and what
changed. (In round 3, notes/08, Ferguson and Shovlin were later swapped out of the bibliography
for primary sources; the findings they supported are still reflected in the paper.)

## Conclusions under test (from draft 1)

1. The EIC was the better investment than the VOC for 1725 → 1745–50.
2. The VOC's fortunes were a decline across 1725–50.
3. The best time to sell the EIC was late 1749–1750; never Dec 1745–1748.
4. Ethics doesn't change the choice; EIC was less harmful in 1725–50.

## Challenge 1 — "The VOC actually out-earned the EIC"

- Searched: VOC vs EIC shareholder returns, 18th c.
- Found: GFD's long-run table (VOC 1719–1794 total return 0.86%/yr, price −2.83%/yr); Ferguson
  (VOC "rose ... to an all-time peak of 786" by 1733).
- Assessment: nothing contradicts conclusion 1. Ferguson *refines* conclusion 2: the VOC rose
  from 650 (1725) to ~786 (c. 1733) before falling. A VOC holder's best exit was ~1733 — outside
  the window. Even if a VOC holder had sold at 786 *plus* 25 years of 18.6% dividends
  (465+786 = 1,251 vs 650 = 1.92x), they would still trail the EIC's central case (2.29x).
- Change: paper now says the VOC "rose, then declined", citing Ferguson [19].

## Challenge 2 — "The VOC rallied when William IV took charge (1749)"

- Searched English and Dutch for a 1749 VOC price rally.
- Found: nothing. De Pinto (who engineered the appointment and would have boasted of a rally)
  reports only that the directors' "despotism" had ended.
- Change: none.

## Challenge 3 — "The EIC was badly damaged by the war in India in the 1740s"

- Found: Mentz (2018): when the fall of Madras was known "the stock fell considerably"; the
  promised ransom (£481,250) was never paid; directors told shareholders losses were only £70,000.
- Assessment: confirms the timing advice (don't sell 1746–47) rather than refuting the choice:
  the Third Report shows the 8% dividend was paid straight through 1746/7 and 1747/8.
- Change: added Mentz quote to "When to sell" [20].

## Challenge 4 — "Holding the EIC longer (into the 1750s) would have been better"

- Found: Shovlin: in 1753–54 Anglo-French company war "damaged trade and threatened their
  solvency" and "stock values fell"; EIC dividend cut to 6% at Christmas 1755 (Third Report).
- Assessment: supports the 1750 exit.
- Change: added Shovlin [21].

## Challenge 5 — "The EIC was the worse actor ethically"

- Considered: EIC slaving voyages (Allen); later conquest of Bengal, 1770 famine; opium to China.
- Assessment: the EIC's large-scale opium-to-China business and territorial rule post-date 1757;
  in 1725–50 the VOC's record (Batavia 1740, slave societies, 1745 opium monopoly) is worse. The
  EIC's slaving is real and is stated in the paper, not hidden.
- Change: none, but the paper keeps the "EIC was not clean" paragraph.

## Challenge 6 — "Your exit prices are made up"

- This is the strongest objection. There is no reachable monthly price series for 1745–50
  (notes/06, sources/s14).
- Response: the paper (a) says so explicitly, (b) uses documented anchors for every scenario
  bound, and (c) shows the ranking holds for *every* combination (worst EIC 2.05x > best VOC 1.54x).
  The *timing* advice relies on documented market-wide movements (3% stock 75 → 101; Bank stock
  117 → 140; Madras "fell considerably"), not on an invented EIC price.
- Residual risk: the exact best month cannot be identified; the paper recommends a window
  (late 1749 – Dec 1750) rather than a day.
