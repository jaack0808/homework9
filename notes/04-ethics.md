# 04 — The ethical question

Question: if the ancestor wants to maximise profit *ethically*, does the choice change?

## What each company was doing with shareholders' money, 1725–1750

### VOC
- **Territorial rule and coercion already in place.** By 1725 the VOC governed Batavia and
  much of Java's north coast, the spice islands (whose monopoly was established by force in
  the 17th century), Ceylon's coast, and the Cape.
- **Batavia massacre, Oct–Nov 1740.** At least 10,000 ethnic Chinese killed; Company soldiers,
  sailors and residents took part; the council offered two ducats per Chinese head.
  Governor-General Valckenier was later condemned (1744). (s09)
- **Slavery at scale.** Allen: the VOC, its employees and Batavian entrepreneurs imported about
  half of the 200,000–300,000 enslaved people who reached Dutch Southeast Asian settlements —
  perhaps as many as 500,000 in total (17th–18th c.). Batavia and the Cape were slave societies. (s07)
- **Opium.** In 1745 the High Government chartered the Amfioen Sociëteit, owned by senior VOC
  servants, to sell Company opium on Java — a guaranteed 1,200 chests a year at a fixed price.
  The stated aim was to suppress smuggling; the effect was an organised drug monopoly run by
  the Company's own officials. (p04)
- **Governance.** De Pinto (1748): directors ignorant of the business, employees' "immense
  thefts" unchecked, the Company "belonged to whoever wanted to plunder it". (p03)

### EIC (before Plassey)
- **Mostly a trading company in 1725–50.** It held forts and factories (Madras, Bombay,
  Calcutta, Bencoolen) under Mughal/local rulers; its era of conquest starts with Arcot (1751)
  and Plassey (1757) — after the ancestor sells.
- **But it used slavery.** Allen: 70 slaving voyages under Company auspices 1622–1772, at least
  3,300 people carried, mostly from Madagascar; probably 10,000–20,000 traded in total; slaves
  worked at Bencoolen and St Helena. (s07)
- **A large part of its value was a loan to the British state** (£3.2m + £1m), paid interest from
  taxes — financing Britain's wars of the 1740s, but not itself an act of colonial violence. (p02)
- **Monopoly critique.** Smith: chartered companies "have both mismanaged and confined" trade;
  later "altogether unfit to govern its territorial possessions". (p05) That verdict concerns
  the post-1757 Company.

## Comparison

| | VOC 1725–50 | EIC 1725–50 |
|---|---|---|
| Mass violence against civilians in the window | Yes (Batavia 1740) | No comparable event in the window |
| Slavery | Large, systemic (Batavia, Cape, Ceylon) | Smaller but real (Bencoolen, St Helena, Madagascar voyages) |
| Drug monopoly | Opium via Amfioen Sociëteit from 1745 | Large-scale Bengal–China opium comes later (after 1757/1773) |
| Territorial rule over subject populations | Yes | Mostly not yet |

## Answer

The choice does **not** change. The EIC is both the more profitable and the less harmful of
the two in 1725–1750. But "less harmful" is not "clean":

1. Every pound of EIC dividend in this period also contains a share of profits from a company
   that bought and worked enslaved people. An ethical investor should know that.
2. The timing advice becomes stronger on ethical grounds: **sell by the end of 1750 and do not
   reinvest** in the EIC, because the next phase (Arcot 1751, Plassey 1757, the Bengal famine of
   1770) is the one in which the Company's profits came from conquest and tax collection.
3. A truly ethics-first ancestor could put part of the money into British 3% government stock or
   Bank of England stock instead — lower return, no direct colonial exposure (though the state
   was itself fighting colonial wars). That is outside the either/or question, so it is a
   footnote in the letter, not the recommendation.
4. One could argue an investor should "use the vote": VOC shareholders petitioned William IV in
   1748–49 to take charge of the Company (De Pinto). But an outside investor with a modest stake
   had no realistic influence on Batavia; the practical ethical lever is where to put the money.

## Things I considered and rejected
- "VOC slavery was a tiny share of its trade value, so it doesn't matter" (a Dutch heritage-site
  claim that slaves were <1% of VOC trade value). Even if true, the Company *governed* slave
  societies; the harm isn't measured by trade turnover. Not cited (weak source).
- "The EIC is worse because it later conquered India." True of the Company's whole history, but
  the question is what the ancestor's money funds in 1725–50, and they exit before 1757.
